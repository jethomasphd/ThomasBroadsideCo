These works speak from different centuries and traditions. We keep their names, their translations, and their sources beside them. The invitation is simple: give the words time.

✦

01 / Homecoming

A long way home.

Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy.

Homer · The Odyssey, Book I · trans. Samuel Butler

The invocation begins with a traveller and a perilous return. Our sheet holds an extract from the opening, with the translator named. Read it slowly; the wider story remains there to be entered.

02 / Mercy

Words to receive with care.

Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy.

Jesus of Nazareth · Matthew 5:7 · Authorized Version

The Beatitudes belong to the opening of the Sermon on the Mount. This verse is one of the blessings; the broadside carries Matthew 5:3–12 together, with verse numbers beside the words.

03 / The soul

The question remains.

For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? or what shall a man give in exchange for his soul?

Jesus of Nazareth · Matthew 16:26 · Authorized Version

Two questions, kept together. The passage asks about the soul and the cost of worldly gain. Its weight belongs to the Gospel itself; the sheet gives it a place to be read and considered.

Read the source ↗Examine the sheet ↗

04 / Rest

A prayer on the page.

Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee.

Augustine · Confessions, Book I · trans. E. B. Pusey

Augustine addresses God in the opening of the Confessions. The restless heart is held within that prayer. We preserve that address, and name the translation beneath the passage.

Read the source ↗Examine the sheet ↗

05 / The way

At the edge of the wood.

Midway upon the journey of our life I found myself within a forest dark, For the straightforward pathway had been lost.

Dante · Inferno, Canto I, lines 1–3 · trans. H. W. Longfellow

The Divine Comedy opens with a lost path. These three lines mark the beginning of a much larger journey. The sheet keeps Longfellow’s line breaks, and gives the source its own line.

Texts, translations & museum records
  1. The Odyssey, Book I

    Homer / translated by Samuel Butler, 1900. The sheet presents an extract from the invocation, ending at “safely home”; the source sentence continues.

  2. The Gospel according to St Matthew

    Authorized Version (King James Version). Matthew 5:3–12 and 16:26. Modern-spelling KJV transcription; 1611 identifies the translation, not a facsimile of its original spelling.

  3. Confessions, Book I

    Augustine / translated by E. B. Pusey. The opening prayer addresses God. The broadside extracts the clause beginning “Thou madest us”.

  4. Inferno, Canto I, lines 1–3

    Dante / translated by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1867. The first tercet. Line breaks and the capital “For” follow the text file used to typeset the existing sheet.

  5. Christ Preaching (The Hundred Guilder Print)

    Rembrandt van Rijn / c. 1649 / National Gallery of Art. Object 9969. Open-access image in the public domain. The work is an etching, drypoint and burin on Japan paper.

  6. Aristotle with a Bust of Homer

    Rembrandt van Rijn / 1653 / The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Object 437394. Public-domain image. Aristotle rests his hand on a bust of Homer.

  7. The Circle of the Lustful: Paolo and Francesca

    William Blake / 1827 / National Gallery of Art. Object 797. Open-access image in the public domain. An engraving after Dante’s Inferno, Canto V.

The Gospel passages are presented as scripture, and Augustine’s words as a prayer addressed to God. The art reproductions preserve the historical artworks. Our broadside compositions are contemporary editions of these texts and images.

About the film & full transcript

The room and landscapes are contemporary visual meditations, imagined for this campaign. They do not document historical places or events. Miriam provides a modern narration; the script identifies the reading from Matthew. No voice is presented as a recording of a historical or sacred figure. The musical score is contemporary.

Transcript. Some words ask us to slow down. To read again. To make room. Homer opens with a traveller, far from home. An old story, still asking for our attention. In Matthew's Gospel, Jesus teaches: Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy. Augustine brings his restless heart to God. A prayer, still held on the page. Dante begins in a dark wood, with the way ahead lost. Poetry. Scripture. Prayer. Art. Distinct works, each with its own source. From our press in Austin, words to live with.
