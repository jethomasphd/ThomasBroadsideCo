The promise of equality in the Declaration coexisted with slavery and exclusion. These sources let us read both the words and their historical setting. This campaign celebrates public access to documents, without presenting the founders as a single voice or an uncomplicated ideal.

About the scenes & film transcript

The opening study and five historical chapter scenes are dramatized contemporary reconstructions. Rooms, gestures, incidental people and actions are imagined; they are not archival footage or recorded events. The narration is a modern account, not the founders’ voices or quotations. The Austin press footage records the actual shop. Music is a contemporary cinematic score.

Film transcript. Before it was a relic, it was news. And news needed a printer. Benjamin Franklin knew the power of print long before he helped shape the Declaration. Thomas Jefferson drafted the words. His colleagues and Congress revised them. On the night of July fourth, seventeen seventy-six, John Dunlap’s shop set the Declaration in type. Copies traveled. On July ninth, Washington had the Declaration read to his troops in New York. A decade later, Hamilton, Madison, and Jay used print to argue for the Constitution as Publius. Different hands. Different roles. One enduring idea: public words must reach the public. We carry that conviction onto our press in Austin. Read the words. Keep the source. Make your own first impression.
