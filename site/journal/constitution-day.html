Constitution Day, and why the Preamble is set in one sentence

The Preamble is fifty-two words and one sentence. We set it that way on purpose.

Most printings break it into lines like verse, which makes it sound like a hymn. It isn't a hymn. It's a working sentence — the longest to-do list ever ratified — and the engrossing clerk, Jacob Shallus, wrote it as one continuous breath with the first three words display-sized. Our sheet keeps his proportions: WE THE PEOPLE large, because that's the subject; everything after in one measure, because that's the predicate, and the country is the object.

The store opens today, on the anniversary of the signing. Digital first: every sheet in the catalog can be downloaded tonight and printed at home for less than the cost of a movie ticket. The press runs next month.

It's a fitting day to open a print shop's store. The Constitution was itself a print job — the Committee of Style sent it to the printers Dunlap and Claypoole, who kept it secret and got it out. We aim to keep only the second half of that tradition.

— J.E.T., from the shop
