01 / Benjamin Franklin / The printer's eye

He knew what a page could do.

The craft, before the founding

Long before the Declaration, Franklin made his living in print. Almanacs, pamphlets, newspapers: useful words, carried into ordinary life.

In June 1776, he brought that practiced attention to the Declaration's draft. The surviving manuscript records changes associated with Franklin and John Adams. The words passed through more than one careful hand.

02 / Thomas Jefferson / The working draft

The words were worked on.

Philadelphia / June–July 1776

Jefferson was the principal draftsman. His colleagues and Congress revised the text before its adoption on July 4. The original Rough draught is full of the marks of that work.

A source lets us see the changes. It gives us the words in context, with the distance between an early draft and the text sent into the world.

03 / John Dunlap / The first impression

A declaration needed a printer.

Philadelphia / July 4–5, 1776

After Congress approved the text, it went to John Dunlap's Philadelphia shop. Through the night of July 4 and into July 5, the Declaration became a printed broadside: a sheet carrying type on one side.

Copies were dispatched to assemblies, committees and military commanders. The first public Declaration was a work of printing. Its words could leave the room where they had been adopted.

04 / George Washington / Words made public

The sheet became a voice.

New York / July 9, 1776

John Hancock sent a printed copy to Washington on July 6. On July 9, Washington had the Declaration read aloud to the troops assembled in New York.

A printed document could be read by one person and heard by many. Paper carried the text; a human voice carried it farther. A fragment of Washington's copy survives in the Library of Congress.

05 / Alexander Hamilton / The argument in print

The argument kept moving.

New York / 1787–1788

The founding did not end in July 1776. During the debate over the Constitution, Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay wrote as Publius, arguing for ratification through print.

Their Federalist essays reached readers through New York newspapers. This was a later debate, with different authors and a different document. Print was again a way to put an argument before the public.

Sources & historical context
  1. The first printing, July 4–5, 1776Library of Congress / National Archives

    The surviving printed broadside and the dispatch of copies on July 5. Historical print-run and survivor counts are intentionally omitted.

  2. Jefferson's original Rough draughtLibrary of Congress, Jefferson Papers

    Draft, alterations by Adams and Franklin, and congressional revisions. The approved text preceded the later engrossed parchment.

  3. Franklin, printer and writerLibrary of Congress

    Primary printed examples of Poor Richard's Almanack and Franklin's printing work.

  4. Washington's copy of the DeclarationLibrary of Congress, George Washington Papers

    Hancock sent the printed Declaration to Washington on July 6; Washington had it read to the troops in New York on July 9.

  5. The Federalist in historic newspapersLibrary of Congress

    Essays by Hamilton, Madison and Jay under Publius, supporting ratification in 1787–1788; the guide indexes original newspaper printings.

  6. Printing the Declaration of IndependenceLibrary of Congress, Rare Book and Special Collections

    The approved wording was sent to Dunlap's shop; the first printing belongs to the evening of July 4 and early July 5.

  7. Declaring Independence: drafting the documentsLibrary of Congress

    The vote for independence on July 2, adoption on July 4, and the later engrossed copy ordered July 19 and signed beginning August 2.

The promise of equality in the Declaration coexisted with slavery and exclusion. These sources let us read both the words and their historical setting. This campaign celebrates public access to documents, without presenting the founders as a single voice or an uncomplicated ideal.

About the scenes & film transcript

The five period scenes are dramatized contemporary reconstructions. Rooms, gestures, incidental people and actions are imagined; they are not archival footage or recorded events. The narration is a modern account, not the founders’ voices or quotations. The Austin press footage records the actual shop. Music is a contemporary cinematic score.
