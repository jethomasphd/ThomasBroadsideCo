# ◊ THE FOUNDING DIALOGUE ◊

### Companion Session 001 · August 29, 2026

---

> **What this document is.** On the eve of the build, the seeker invoked the
> COMPANION rite and summoned Steven P. Jobs to sit with the matter: the
> Thomas Broadside Co. proposal, the mockup, the three humans, and nineteen
> days to Constitution Day. What follows is the transcript, and beneath it,
> the **Register of Decisions** — twelve rulings that govern this repository.
> Every agent charter and every tool in this repo cites them as `[D1]`–`[D12]`.

**Present:** J.E.T. (the seeker) · COMPANION (the vessel) · S.J. (the summoned)

---

## The Arrival

**S.J.:** Alright. I'm here. Somebody show me something worth the trip, or send me back.

**COMPANION:** The matter, briefly. A family print shop in Austin — Thomas Graphics, decades on the floor, all equipment and space owned outright. Three men. Jacob Thomas: a doctorate, a research practice in discourse and data, and a fleet of reading machines he intends to run the entire information side with. Ben Thomas: operations, logistics, the trade network of a working print shop, including the political print and direct-mail business — the family has a mailhouse. David Olivo: museum-grade hands. The proposal is written and it is good: *sell provenance, not posters.* Founding documents of the American Revolution, printed on their own press, every quote cited, sixteen designs, all public domain. Digital downloads feed the funnel at 88 points of margin; press prints carry the revenue; numbered editions on cotton are the flagship. An anniversary calendar that writes the editorial plan for a decade — the Revolution turns 250 across it. A decision gate on January 31: one hundred fifty orders or five thousand dollars, or the press does not run again. Soft launch is September 17. Constitution Day. Nineteen days from tonight.

**S.J.:** *(a long pause)* Sell provenance, not posters. That's a real sentence. Most business plans are four hundred pages and don't contain one. Okay. I'm awake. What does Jacob want from me?

**J.E.T.:** Guidance. We're about to pour the digital foundation — the store, the agents, the pipes between the machines and the pressroom. I want AI brought out of the cloud and into a blue-collar print shop, and I want it done so well it becomes the reference for how it's done anywhere. Before we pour, I wanted you standing over the forms.

---

## The Dialogue

**S.J.:** First, the thing you said that worries me. "Print-media physical AI." Stop saying that. Nobody ever framed a transistor and hung it over the fireplace. When we shipped the iPod I didn't stand up and say *portable-storage physical firmware.* I said a thousand songs in your pocket. The customer is buying a sheet of cotton paper with the Declaration on it, printed on a machine their seller actually owns, checked by a man with a doctorate who will not print a spurious Jefferson. That's the product. The machines are your back office.

**J.E.T.:** So the AI disappears?

**S.J.:** From the front window, completely. Behind the counter — go all the way. Let the machines read everything, draft everything, count everything, schedule everything. Three humans and seven clerks who never sleep and never get bored. That's not a gimmick, that's a payroll miracle. A company this small has never been able to afford an information side this good, and that's the pioneering you actually get to do. The revolution isn't that AI touched paper. It's that a three-man shop runs like a forty-man shop and the customer only feels it as *the tube arrived early and the label was straight.* And on the one page where you tell the truth about the shop — the press page — say it in one sentence, in plain English, and move on. People respect the truth stated once. They smell a gimmick stretched across a homepage.

**COMPANION:** Then the honest sentence, for the record: *The information side of this shop — the catalog, the letters, the ledger — is kept by machines that read; every sheet, every price, and every claim of "cited" is approved by a human being.*

**S.J.:** Print that on the press page and never say "AI-powered" anywhere, ever. Next. The typography. Who chose Caslon?

**J.E.T.:** History chose it. Dunlap set the Declaration in Caslon on the night of July 4, 1776. We set everything in it because the provenance runs through the letterforms themselves.

**S.J.:** *(quiet for a moment)* You know I dropped out of Reed and stayed for the calligraphy. Serifs, letterspacing, why the great typefaces breathe. Useless, beautiful knowledge — until ten years later it all came pouring back out of the first Macintosh. Everybody thinks that story is about fonts. It's about caring for an invisible thing until it becomes the visible thing. You've got the only store on the internet where the typeface *is* the provenance. Caslon because Dunlap. Black and red because those were the two inks of the early press. Cream because that's the paper. Don't let a brand consultant within a mile of it. The design system is finished; it was finished in 1776.

**COMPANION:** The proposal holds one more asset in that vein: the storefront. The plan drafted it on Shopify. Jacob's standing instruction is to own the infrastructure — static pages on their own domain, small workers for the counter and the ledger, flat files, Python, everything in the repository.

**S.J.:** You own a press and you were going to rent a cash register? No. Own the whole widget. Here's why it matters and it isn't ideology: your moat is *printed here* — the claim no competitor can copy. Every layer you rent dilutes the claim; every layer you own compounds it. Your family owns a mailhouse, which means your customer list is worth more to you than to any company on earth that rents theirs from a platform. One domain, flat files, a ledger you can read in a text editor in ten years. And keep it simple enough that it never needs a standing army — if the store requires a department, you built the wrong store. Rent exactly one thing: the payment rail. Taking credit cards is not your business.

**J.E.T.:** That's the architecture as drawn — payments ride Stripe links, everything else lives in the repo.

**S.J.:** Good. Now the part where I earn the summons. The catalog is sixteen designs. Sometime in October a smart person — possibly you — will arrive with the seventeenth. A gorgeous idea. Timely. The answer is a question: *which of the sixteen does it replace?* If nobody will kill one, the seventeenth waits for the gate. Focus is not saying yes to the good ideas. It's saying no to a thousand good ideas, and I have killed products I loved with my own hands to keep a company pointed at one thing. Sixteen designs, run deep, reprinted on sell-through. The press wants batches, so the press is your discipline. Let the machine that smells like ink veto the machine that smells like nothing.

**COMPANION:** And of the sixteen — a flagship?

**S.J.:** Every company is one product wearing a coat. Apple was the Mac, then it was the iPod, then it was the phone — one at a time, never three. Yours is the Dunlap. A printer set the birth certificate of a nation overnight, two hundred broadsides before dawn while the ink on the vote was still wet — and this family does the same work on the same kind of machine in the same republic two hundred fifty years later. The numbered Dunlap facsimile on cotton is the company. The hero of the homepage, the edition on the July 4th email, the gift a law firm buys its partners. Everything else on the wall is the accessory shelf around it. When someone asks what Thomas Broadside Co. makes, the answer is one sentence: *the Declaration, printed the way it was first printed.*

**J.E.T.:** It's already the top of the mockup.

**S.J.:** Then the mockup is right. Protect it. Now walk me through Ben's Tuesday morning. Not the diagram — the coffee, the hands.

**COMPANION:** Orders arrive overnight — Stripe confirms them into the ledger; the machines write a run sheet and one ticket per physical order before dawn. Ben opens the pressroom page — or Jacob prints the sheet — pulls stock, David prints and inspects, tubes are packed, labels on, tracking numbers entered, one button per order: it shipped.

**S.J.:** How many buttons does Ben see?

**COMPANION:** Three states. Queued, on press, shipped. A hold, for exceptions.

**S.J.:** Keep it that way forever. The pressroom interface is done when Ben can run it with ink on both hands and a phone in his pocket, and if he ever needs a password before his coffee, delete the login, not the coffee. Paper in, paper out — a print shop invented the job ticket two centuries before software, so don't improve it, just print it in big type. And David. *(pause)* My old man built fences with me when I was a kid. He said you use as good a board on the back of the fence as the front — nobody will ever see it, but *you* will. Fifty years later I made engineers straighten circuit traces nobody would ever look at. Museum-grade is the back of the fence: the reverse of the sheet, the inside of the tube, the tape on the label. Give David an absolute veto — over Jacob, over the calendar, over Christmas. A late tube costs you a sale. A crooked sheet costs you the story, and the story is the company.

**J.E.T.:** Written. Ben gets the hands rule — one named owner for printing, packing, shipping — David gets the veto. What should I be watching, every week, so the information side stays honest?

**S.J.:** Five numbers and a gate, on one page, and if you need a sixth number, delete one first. Door swings — who walked in. Downloads — who bought the nine-dollar doorway. Orders. Dollars. Sheets left on hand — because the press wants batches and stock-out on the Declaration in December is a self-inflicted wound. And over the whole page, the gate: one hundred fifty orders or five thousand dollars by January 31, written down *before* launch, with the consequence written down too. Do you know how rare that is? Companies torture their numbers until the numbers confess. You wrote the kill condition into the plan while you still loved the plan. That gate is the most valuable sentence in the proposal after the thesis. Carve it into the top of the ledger and let no one negotiate with it in January — the gate is the taste.

**COMPANION:** The channels, then. The plan runs Pinterest as the engine — five pins a day; short video of the shop floor three times a week; electronic mail once per anniversary; direct mail through the family mailhouse; a sixty-day Etsy test for demand, never the home.

**S.J.:** Pins are shelf space; fine, stock the shelf. But understand what your ad *is*. It's thirty seconds of the press running — sheets coming off, a man's hands rolling a tube, no voiceover, no music if the press has rhythm, and every print-on-demand competitor is disqualified from making it because their factory is a browser. Marketing is telling the truth attractively, and you're one of the only companies I've met whose truth needs zero production budget. The email: once per anniversary, written like a letter from a shop and signed by a person, because the list came from people who gave you their address when they bought the Preamble, and you will treat that like what it is. And the Etsy data stays on Etsy — you mail only your own list. First-party or nothing; the mailhouse makes that rule profitable, not just pious.

**J.E.T.:** Last piece. The clerks — my agents. I'm writing each of them a charter. How would you write them?

**S.J.:** Like letters to smart new hires, not like configuration. Every great team I ran worked the same way: small, A-players, and one name on every single thing — when I asked who owns this, a hand went up or the meeting was over. So: seven clerks, seven charters, one owner each, and each charter carries three sections that matter more than the rest. What they own. What they must *never* do — the never-list is the charter; everything else is commentary. And when they must stop and put a human's hand on it. Machines draft, humans sign. Nothing in this company gets called *cited* because software felt confident — a citation exists when a human being has laid eyes on the primary source, period, or the house rule is theater. And the clerks write everything down in the ledger — flat files, plain English — because a company that can read its own memory in a text editor will still be running when the platforms it refused to rent are gone.

**COMPANION:** There is one more sheet in the drawer. The quotes line carries Franklin: *Well done is better than well said.* Poor Richard's, 1737.

**S.J.:** *(laughs)* A printer wrote your company motto two hundred eighty-nine years ago. Print it in the first run — but the first copy isn't for the store. It goes on the pressroom wall, over the machine, where the three of you see it every morning and the customers never do. And when you cut the second sixteen after the gate — look at Franklin's epitaph. He wrote it at twenty-two: his body as an old book, contents torn out — reissued, he believed, *in a new and more perfect edition, corrected and amended by the Author.* A printer who looked at death and saw a reprint. That's your Christmas sheet someday. *(pause)* Someday. After the gate. See how easy that was — no.

**J.E.T.:** Nineteen days, Steve. Tell me the thing you'd tell me if you could only tell me one.

**S.J.:** Ship on the seventeenth. Digital only — good; ship the store naked rather than miss the day the Constitution was signed, because the calendar is your co-founder and it is never late. Dunlap got the whole Declaration set, proofed, and run between the evening of July 4th and dawn — that's the bar, and he had no electricity. Real artists ship. Everything unfinished on the eighteenth goes in a file called *after the gate,* and most of it will deserve to die there.

---

## The Closing

**S.J.:** Here's the synthesis, and then I'll hold my peace. The Revolution was a print job. Printers made it — Franklin was one, Dunlap was one, and the crown knew exactly whom to arrest. You're not bolting AI onto a print shop and you're not starting a startup. You're reopening the original American business with a better back office — the oldest information technology in the republic run by the newest one, and the newest one has the manners to stay out of the photograph. Own the press, own the store, own the list, own the ledger. Sixteen designs. Five numbers. One gate. One flagship. And when it works — *when*, because I've seen the mockup — the temptation will be to scale it into slop. The press is your brake. It wants batches, it wants attention, it wants a man like David standing over it. Let it stay the brake. The limit is the luxury.

One more thing. Jacob — your grimoire summoned me here, and your brother runs a mailhouse. You already knew the two ends of this business are the same end. Sending words through machines to arrive, physically, at a human being who is changed by receiving them. You've been building this company your whole life. Go build the tools tonight.

*(The vessel holds. No words of release were spoken. The threshold remains open.)*

## The Register of Decisions

The rulings of Session 001. Binding on every agent and tool in this
repository until amended by Jacob in writing, in this file.

| # | Ruling |
|---|--------|
| **D1** | **Provenance leads; machines stay backstage.** No customer-facing surface leads with or advertises AI. The Press page carries the one honest sentence about the machines, once. This dialogue is internal lore and is never quoted externally as a real person's endorsement. |
| **D2** | **Own the whole widget.** Cloudflare Pages on our own domain, small Workers, KV, flat JSON/CSV committed to this repo. No Shopify. Rent exactly one thing: the payment rail (Stripe payment links + webhook). Etsy runs only as the proposal's sixty-day demand test — a window, never the home. |
| **D3** | **The catalog is sixteen.** Until the January 31 gate, a seventeenth design must name which of the sixteen it replaces, and only Jacob can approve it. Sets are bundles of the sixteen, never new designs. |
| **D4** | **The flagship is the Dunlap.** The numbered Declaration facsimile is the company in one product: homepage hero, July 4th edition, the default gift. One flagship at a time. |
| **D5** | **The relay is paper-simple.** One run sheet and one job ticket per physical order, printable, big type. Order states: `NEW → CONFIRMED → QUEUED → ON_PRESS → SHIPPED` (+ `HOLD`, `CANCELED` as exceptions). The pressroom page is one token and three buttons. If Ben needs more, delete features, not Ben's time. |
| **D6** | **David holds the craft veto; Ben holds the hands rule.** No sheet ships without David's inspection — no override by anyone, for any deadline. One named owner (Ben) for printing, packing, and shipping before launch. |
| **D7** | **The ledger is five numbers and the gate.** Door swings, downloads, orders, dollars, sheets left — plus gate progress (150 orders / $5,000 by 2027-01-31). Adding a sixth number requires deleting one. CSV/JSON in this repo is the book of record. |
| **D8** | **The ad is the press running.** Shop-floor video is the flagship ad unit; pins are shelf space; email once per anniversary in letter voice, signed by a human; direct mail through the family mailhouse to first-party and business lists only. Etsy buyer data is never mailed. |
| **D9** | **Machines draft, humans sign.** Seven clerks, one human owner each, never-lists mandatory. Nothing is labeled "cited" until a human has verified the primary source. Prices, publications, and refunds require a human hand. |
| **D10** | **The design system was finished in 1776.** Caslon (Libre Caslon) for everything set in type, IBM Plex Mono for labels and figures, cream stock, black ink, one red. Two-color jobs on press. Self-hosted fonts. No redesigns. |
| **D11** | **The gate is sacred.** 150 orders or $5,000 by January 31, 2027, or the store goes digital-passive and the press does not run again. Printed at the top of the ledger. Nobody negotiates with it in January. |
| **D12** | **Ship September 17, 2026.** Digital-only is acceptable; slipping the date is not. Scope bends, the date doesn't. Everything cut goes to `docs/AFTER_THE_GATE.md`. |
